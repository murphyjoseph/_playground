# Domain Profile: Scientific / Medical / Empirical

For questions about what the evidence says: health claims, interventions, empirical findings, "state of the research on X."

## Source tiers (strongest first)

1. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Cochrane is the gold standard; PRISMA-compliant reviews generally)
2. GRADE-rated clinical guidelines (WHO, NICE, major specialty bodies)
3. Individual RCTs — preregistered ones (ClinicalTrials.gov, PROSPERO) weigh more
4. Cohort and other observational studies
5. Case reports, mechanistic reasoning, expert opinion
6. Preprints (medRxiv/bioRxiv) — usable for recency, but always label as un-refereed
7. Science journalism — only as a pointer to the underlying papers; cite the papers, not the coverage

## GRADE-lite certainty rating

Study design is a prior, not a verdict. Start bodies of RCT evidence at "high" certainty and observational evidence at "low," then adjust:

**Downgrade for:** risk of bias (no blinding/randomization problems, no preregistration), inconsistency across studies, indirectness (surrogate outcomes, wrong population), imprecision (small samples, wide intervals), suspected publication bias.

**Upgrade observational evidence for:** very large effect sizes, dose-response gradients, plausible confounding that would work *against* the observed effect.

A rigorous large observational study can outrank a flawed RCT. State the resulting certainty (high / moderate / low / very low) alongside the finding.

## Replication-crisis rules

- **A single study is weak evidence, full stop** — in the landmark reproducibility project, only ~36% of significant psychology findings replicated. When only single studies exist, say so and downgrade confidence explicitly.
- Weight replicated, meta-analyzed, and preregistered findings far above novel single results.
- Positive quality markers: preregistration, registered reports, open data/code, large consortium studies.
- Beware correlation→causation leaps in observational work: plausible confounds are far more common than intuition suggests.
- Check whether load-bearing papers have been retracted or corrected (Retraction Watch database; retractions now exceed 10,000/year). A retracted paper's claims are void regardless of how often they're still cited.

## Journal legitimacy: allowlists, not blacklists

Blacklists (Beall's-list mirrors) are stale and prone to false positives. Check positive signals instead:

- Indexed in **DOAJ** (ideally with the DOAJ Seal), **Scopus**, or **Web of Science/JCR** — indexing takes ~a year to earn, making it the strongest single signal
- **COPE** membership; passes the **Think·Check·Submit** checklist
- Identifiable editorial board with real, verifiable affiliations; transparent peer-review and fee policies

**Predatory red flags:** guaranteed or 24-hour "peer review"; names mimicking prestigious journals; fake metrics ("Global Impact Factor," "SJIF," "Index Copernicus Value" — the only legitimate Impact Factor comes from Clarivate's JCR); site content aimed at soliciting authors rather than serving readers.

If a journal appears only on a blacklist mirror, verify current Scopus/WoS/DOAJ indexing before discarding it.

## Hard rules for this domain

- Never use impact factor or journal prestige as a proxy for an individual paper's quality (citation distributions are too skewed; this is the DORA consensus).
- Never present preprint findings without the un-refereed label.
- When guidelines and primary literature conflict, check guideline recency — guidelines lag; recent high-certainty evidence can supersede them, but say you're making that judgment.
- Distinguish "no evidence of effect" from "evidence of no effect" — absence of studies is not a negative result.
